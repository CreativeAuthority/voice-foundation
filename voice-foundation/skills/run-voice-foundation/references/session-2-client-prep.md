# Session 2 — Canonical Voice

Creative Authority · Operating Foundation

This is the second session. The first one taught your AI how you decide. This one teaches it how you sound.

Like the first, we work through it together in one guided conversation. The questions are here so you can prepare. This session runs better with evidence than with memory, so bring real material. Pull two or three emails you actually sent and are proud of, and have a recent client conversation fresh in your mind. We will use your real words, not invented ones.

## What this session is really doing

Voice is not a tone setting. It is the specific way you put things, and it lives in two different places that most people collapse into one. Your written voice is in your emails, market updates, and posts. Your spoken voice is in listing appointments, buyer consults, objection handling, and follow-up calls. They are not the same, and your AI needs both, each with its own examples and its own limits.

This session captures both and turns them into a file that loads alongside your operating context. From then on, your AI can draft an email that reads like you wrote it and build a script that sounds like you said it, without borrowing anyone else's language.

One thing to know going in: this session does not repeat your decision rules. Those live in the file from Session 1. This one is only about how things sound.

## How to prepare

The strongest input is real text. Before we meet, find two or three emails you have already sent that sound unmistakably like you. Do not write anything new for this. Recall a recent consult or call, ideally one where you handled a real objection well. The examples you bring carry more weight than any answer you could compose.

## Two ways to answer

This session has a written track and a spoken track, and the way you answer each one is different on purpose.

For the written questions, type your answers the way you normally write. For the spoken questions, do not type. Speak them out loud using voice-to-text and let it transcribe. Tap the mic, say it the way you would actually say it to a client, and leave it as it comes out.

Here is why it matters. The moment you type a spoken answer, you start editing yourself into written prose, and the real rhythm of how you talk disappears. Dictation keeps your pauses, your phrasing, and the way you actually build a thought out loud. Do not clean up the transcript. The unpolished version is the one we want. Every spoken question below is marked so you know which ones to say instead of type.

## Part 1 — Who you are talking to

Written: Who are you most often writing to, and what do you want them to feel and do next? Describe the person specifically. Their situation, what they know, how they feel when your message lands. Then name the feeling you want to leave and the action you want them to take.

Spoken · say this one out loud, use voice-to-text: Who are you most often speaking with, in what setting, and what emotional state do you want them in when the conversation ends? Buyer consult, listing appointment, follow-up, a hard objection call. Name the context and the outcome you steer toward.

## Part 2 — How you earn trust

Written: When you give advice in writing, what do you lead with, and what will you never write? Facts, experience, a principle, a question? Then name what is off-limits: hype, urgency, vague promises, exaggeration.

Spoken · say this one out loud, use voice-to-text: How do you open a conversation to establish trust, and how do you handle it when a client pushes back? What are the first words out of your mouth? When someone gets skeptical, do you slow down, ask, reframe, acknowledge? Give a real example. Then name the moves or scripts you would never use.

## Part 3 — How you make things clear

Written: Read me a real trade-off explanation you have already written. An email, a market update, a client note. Something real, not composed for this. This becomes your written explanation anchor.

Spoken · say this one out loud, use voice-to-text: Walk a client through a hard trade-off out loud, the way you actually say it. Pricing, an offer below asking, waiving an inspection, DIY versus hiring it out. Say it as dialogue, not a summary.

Then, still speaking out loud: what are the two or three objections you handle most often, and how do you actually respond to each? Give me your real response, not the ideal one. This feeds directly into your role-play and script material, so say it the way a client would hear it.

## Part 4 — Your actual language

Bring your emails and your recent conversation to this part. Use phrases you truly use. Do not invent them.

Written: The words and phrases you naturally reach for. The words and phrases you avoid because they feel hype-y, vague, or not you. Your typical sentence rhythm, and what your writing looks like when it goes wrong, meaning what AI-generated text sounds like when it is clearly not you.

Spoken · say this one out loud, use voice-to-text: The phrases you naturally use in conversation. The phrases or scripts you would never say because they feel false or pushy. How you pace and deliver, how you open when you sit down with a new client, and how you close a call. Where you can, do not describe your phrases, just say them the way you say them.

## Part 5 — What AI may do with your voice

This is narrower than your Session 1 boundaries. It is only about writing and speaking.

Written: What can AI help you with in writing, and what must it never do? Structuring, clarifying, outlining, expanding a draft you started. Then the limits: changing your intent, adding urgency, inventing specifics, using hype, padding with filler.

Spoken: What can AI help you with in scripts and role-plays, and what must it never do? Building scripts, objection frameworks, consult structures, talking points. Then the limits: closing pressure, manipulation, lines that sound scripted. This one is about permissions rather than voice, so type it if that is easier.

And: when AI is not sure whether something sounds like you, what do you want it to do? Ask, flag the assumption, show you options?

## Part 6 — Your gold-standard examples

These matter more than anything else in the session. They are the primary evidence your AI uses to understand your voice. Do not compose them. Find things that already exist.

Written anchor: one short written example, roughly six to twelve sentences, that shows your voice at its best. A client email, a market update, a newsletter paragraph, a post. Something real you are proud of.

Spoken anchor · say this one out loud, use voice-to-text: a spoken example that shows how you sound at your best. A voicemail you would leave, a consult opening you use every time, an objection response you have refined over years. Do not write it. Say it as if the client were in front of you, and leave the transcript as it comes out. This is the single most important input in the session.

Optional but valuable, and best said out loud: a conversation that went wrong, where it did not land. What was missing? The negative example is often the most instructive one.

---

## What you walk away with

Your answers are assembled into your canonical voice file. It holds your written voice and your spoken voice as two distinct systems, each with its rules, its real examples, and its limits. It loads with your operating context, so everything your AI produces starts from your actual voice instead of a generic default.

Your written voice and your spoken voice are not the same, and they should not be. But both should be unmistakably yours. That is authorship. AI can help you move faster. This is what keeps you the author.
